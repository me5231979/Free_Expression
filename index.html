    Lesson 1 of 6 · The commitment

    Keep the conditions for inquiry.

    The university’s commitment is not merely to allow disagreement, but to maintain an environment in which disagreement and intellectual exploration can occur.
    What this asks of staff

    For staff, this commitment creates an important institutional responsibility: helping preserve the conditions in which students and faculty can pursue those activities, even when the ideas being expressed are controversial, unpopular, or contested.

    Free expression, open inquiry, and civil discourse are therefore not separate commitments. Together, they describe both the kind of intellectual community Vanderbilt seeks to foster and the shared responsibility for sustaining it.

    Lesson 2 of 6 · Free expression

    Principle 1 of 3

    Protect is not endorse.

    Protecting expression is not the same as endorsing expression.

    Students and faculty will encounter ideas with which they strongly disagree. Maintaining an environment for free expression does not require Vanderbilt, or an individual staff member, to agree with those ideas.

    The working definition
    • The freedom to express, examine, question, and challenge ideas, including ideas that others may find disagreeable or offensive.
    • At a university, this principle is especially important to the work of students and faculty. Learning and scholarship require room to ask difficult questions, investigate competing explanations, challenge arguments, develop new ideas, and respond to the ideas of others.
    • Vanderbilt’s Freedom of Expression policy emphasizes that when people encounter ideas contrary to their own, the response should generally be discussion, debate, and mutually respectful dialogue rather than suppression.

    Lesson 3 of 6 · Our responsibility as staff

    Not agreement. Conditions.

    Staff play an essential role in creating and maintaining the conditions in which students’ learning, teaching, research, inquiry, and exchange of ideas can flourish.

    Our responsibility is to help students and faculty
    • Ask difficult questions.
    • Encounter unfamiliar ideas.
    • Express disagreement.
    • Challenge arguments.
    • Engage one another with dignity and respect.
    • Participate in university programs and activities without disruption.
    • Exercise their own expressive rights while respecting the expressive rights of others.

    Lesson 4 of 6 · Time, place, and manner

    Ask the right question.

    Free expression and reasonable rules about conduct can coexist.

    The key question is generally not“Do I agree with this message?”

    Instead, ask“Is the expression occurring in a way that is consistent with university policy and allows students, faculty, and others to continue speaking, listening, teaching, learning, researching, participating, and accessing university activities?”

    Lesson 4 of 6 · Why context matters

    Both rights, at the same time.

    A protest directed at an ongoing event is different, because the expressive rights of the protesters must coexist with the rights of others to participate in that event.

    the right to protestandthe right of others to speak, listen, participate, and learn
    Demonstration or protest: the difference

    During a demonstration that is independent of another event, audible and symbolic activities such as speeches, chanting, marching, and signs may generally occur consistent with university policies.

    During protests and counterprotests, silent and symbolic activities, such as picketing, holding signs, turning backs, or covering ears, may occur, while audible activity may be restricted when it would interfere with the targeted event.

    Planning, and spontaneous expression

    Vanderbilt encourages organizers to work with the Vice Provost and Dean of Students or designee in advance so demonstrations and protests can occur as envisioned with minimal complications. When possible, organizers should provide at least 48 hours’ notice of the planned time, date, and location.

    Spontaneous expression is also addressed by university policy. Dissent, by its nature, may occur spontaneously without advance university review. Other spontaneous activism may be subject to relocation or termination so that organizers can work through the planning process.

    Lesson 5 of 6 · What is the staff role?

    Conduct, not viewpoint.

    Staff do not need to become free-expression policy experts or personally adjudicate protests. When staff encounter expressive activity:

    1. Focus on conduct, not viewpoint.
    2. Do not assume controversy or offense means the expression violates policy.
    3. Consider whether others can still enter, hear, participate, learn, work, and move safely through the space.
    4. Do not independently attempt to resolve a protest or demonstration outside your role.
    5. Know the appropriate university contact or escalation pathway.
    6. Follow the direction of designated university officials.
    7. Avoid unnecessary escalation.

    Closing

    A culture to sustain.

    Free expression at Vanderbilt is not simply a policy to know. It is a culture the university community has a responsibility to sustain.

    For staff, that means helping create an environment where students can encounter ideas, ask difficult questions, disagree, listen, test arguments, and develop their own conclusions, while ensuring that others can continue to learn, speak, participate, and access university programs.

    Plain-language glossary
    Free expression
    The freedom to express, examine, question, and challenge ideas, including ones others find disagreeable or offensive.
    Open forum
    Any space or opportunity where different ideas can be presented, questioned, debated, and tested.
    Civil discourse
    Effective, respectful dialogue across difference. It does not require agreement.
    Endorse
    To agree with or approve of an idea. Protecting someone’s expression does not endorse it.
    Demonstration
    Expression independent of another campus event or activity.
    Protest
    Expression intentionally connected to another campus event or activity.
    Counterprotest
    Expression responding to another organizer’s activity.
    Dissent
    A short, spontaneous, nonviolent verbal or nonverbal reaction to a speaker.
    Counterexpression
    Answering speech with more speech: a sign, a question, an alternative event.
    Time, place, and manner
    Reasonable rules about when, where, and how expression happens. They do not depend on what the message says.
    Viewpoint
    What a person believes or says. Staff respond to conduct, never to viewpoint.
    Conduct
    What a person does: blocking a door, drowning out a speaker, damaging property.
    Disruption
    Conduct that materially interferes with teaching, events, access, or other authorized university activities.
    Community Creed
    The student-developed statement of Vanderbilt values: Academic, Neighborly, Courageous, Honest, Open, Respectful.
    Escalation pathway
    The person or office you contact when expressive activity needs a university response.
